# Reach

AVOS makes the video. Whether anyone watches it is decided by about four things, none of which are the video.

## The first second

A short is kept or dropped in roughly a second, before the story has started. So shot 1 is not an establishing shot,
it is the hook — and the establishing shot, if you need one, goes second.

A hook is one of:

- **The end, first.** The finished dish, the fanned slices, the toy fully transformed. Then cut back to the start.
- **A question the frame can't answer.** Something mid-action with no context yet.
- **The strangest frame in the film.** Whatever a stranger would stop on.

What is not a hook: a title card, a logo, a slow fade in, a wide shot of a room. AVOS's skills all end on their best
frame; for a short, move it to the front and let the rest play out.

## One film, several shapes

A finished AVOS run is a set of clips, so re-cutting costs nothing but time.

| Shape | What changes |
|---|---|
| **9:16, 15–30s** | the hook first, every shot shorter, the last beat at the end with no settle |
| **1:1, 30s** | same cut, centre-safe: check nothing important sits in the top or bottom eighth |
| **16:9, 45–60s** | the original, with the establishing shot restored and the beats allowed to breathe |
| **A still** | the single best frame, which is often not from the shot you expect |

Make the 16:9 first and cut down. Cutting down keeps the story; cutting up pads it.

**Safe margins.** Anything in the bottom sixth of a vertical video sits under the caption and the buttons on most
apps. AVOS puts its own watermark there deliberately; put nothing else you need read.

## The words around it

More reach is lost here than in the video.

- **Title.** Say the thing, not the category. "50 cuts through a papaya" travels; "ASMR video" does not.
- **First line of the caption.** It is the only line most people see. Make it the question the video answers.
- **No spoiler in the thumbnail text** if the video has a reveal — and no reveal in the first frame if the hook is
  the question.
- **One subject per video.** A video about two things is a video about nothing.

## Planning a run, not a one-off

One good video is luck. The useful unit is a **series**: one format, one look, many subjects.

That is what AVOS is shaped for — a skill is a format, and the subject is the only thing you change. Pick one skill,
write twenty subjects, make them in a batch. The look is already locked, so they will read as a set, and a set is
what builds an audience.

Good series shapes, with the skill that makes them:

| Series | Skill | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| One object, N cuts, every week | **ASMR Cutting** | free, deterministic, and the counter is the format |
| One number explained in ten seconds | **Motion Graphics** | free, and the number is the hook |
| One company, one turn in its history | **Company Story** | the research is the moat |
| One dish, start to plated | **ASMR Cooking** | the sound is the format |
| One idea, one of 66 looks | **Film Style** | the look is the series, the subject rotates |

**Cost decides the cadence.** Check **Analytics** before committing to a weekly series: at Seedance Mini rates a
60-second Company Story is about $9.30, so a weekly one is roughly $40 a month, while a weekly ASMR Cutting or Motion
Graphics is **free**. A series you can afford to finish beats a better one you abandon.

## What not to bother with

- Posting the same cut to four platforms at once. Make the vertical properly or don't post it vertically.
- Chasing a trend with a format that takes a day to make.
- More videos. Twenty forgettable ones do less than four that look like they belong together.
